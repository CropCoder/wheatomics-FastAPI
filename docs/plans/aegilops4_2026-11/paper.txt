Chromosome-level reference genome assemblies and
annotation of four diploid Aegilops
Bijendra Khadka · Tara Edwards · Madeleine Lévesque-Lemay · Jennifer Ens ·
Harmeet S. Chawla · Chunfang Zheng · Frank M. You · Curtis J. Pozniak · Sylvie Cloutier

Received: 6 November 2025 / Accepted: 22 September 2026
© Crown 2026

Abstract
Background Aegilops species represent an important reservoir of genetic diversity for agronomic and end-use
quality traits as well as resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses for wheat improvement. However, the limited
availability of high-quality reference genomes has impeded the effective use of these wild relatives in breeding
programs.
Results Here, we present chromosome-level reference genome assemblies of four diploid Aegilops species: Ae.
comosa, Ae. markgrafii, Ae. umbellulata and Ae. uniaristata. Using a hybrid strategy combining Pacific Biosci-
ence (PacBio) HiFi sequencing, BioNano optical mapping and Hi-C scaffolding, we generated contiguous assem-
blies ranging from 3.58 to 4.48 Gb characterized by BUSCO completeness score ranging from 97.9 to 98.4%.
RNASeq and IsoSeq data from multiple tissues were integrated to facilitate gene annotation. The annotation
yielded a total of 47,246 to 51,203 protein-coding genes across the four species, while also characterizing the
repeat element composition of these diploid Aegilops. Collinearity analyses identified conserved colinear blocks
and revealed multiple intra- and inter-chromosomal rearrangements. Orthogroups shared among the Aegilops and
Triticum species were used to perform a phylogenetic analysis that clarified evolutionary relationships among the
genomes of this Aegilops/Triticum complex, where the diploid genomes of these four species clustered as a sister
monophyletic clade within the D-lineage.
Conclusions This study produced the reference-quality genome assemblies of four diploid Aegilops species,
completing the set of high-quality assemblies now available for all 11 diploid Aegilops species and establishing
foundational genomic resources for comparative genomics, gene discovery, and the acceleration of wheat breed-
ing strategies. Additionally, these genomic resources offer a critical framework for extending genomic character-
ization of tetraploid and hexaploid Aegilops species.

Background

The genus Aegilops harbors a plethora of valuable genes that can increase wheat genetic diversity and contrib-
ute to its improvement [1–3]. Aegilops species played a pivotal role in shaping the evolution of common wheat
Triticum aestivum (2n = 6x = 42, ABD genome). Notably, the D and B subgenomes originated from the diploid
Ae. tauschii [4, 5] species and a species closely related to Ae. speltoides, respectively [6–9]. The Aegilops genus
is comprised of 23 species, including two with subspecies, classified into five sections, of which 11 are diploid
(2n = 2x = 14), 10 are tetraploid (2n= 4x = 28), and four are hexaploid (2n= 6x = 42) [10]. The diploid Aegilops
species contain the S, Sb, Sl, Ss, Ssh, U, C, D, M, N, and T genomes that contributed to the creation and evolution
of the tetraploid and hexaploid species through natural hybridization [11, 12]. Ae. tauschii (D) and Ae. speltoides
(S) are considered to belong to the primary gene pool of T. aestivum, while the remaining belongs to its tertiary
gene pool [13]. Genes governing agronomic traits, biotic and abiotic stresses have been transferred from Aegilops
species to common wheat [2, 14]. Thus far, these species have only modestly contributed to broadening wheat’s
genetic diversity. Genomics resources, such as high-quality reference genome assemblies and annotation, are
expected to spur the utilization of these genetic resources for wheat improvement.
   To date, genome assemblies have been released for nine diploid Aegilops species: Ae. tauschii [15, 16], Ae.
speltoides [17–19], Ae. longissima [17–19], Ae. comosa [20], Ae. bicornis [18], Ae. searsii [18], Ae. sharonensis
[18, 21], Ae. umbellulata [22, 23] and Ae. mutica [24]. Fragmented/partial assemblies of Ae. mutica [18] and Ae.
comosa [25] were also released. These reference genomes were used to clone rust resistance genes Lr47 from Ae.
speltoides [26], Lr9 from Ae. umbellulata [27] and Sr62 from Ae. sharonensis [21]. Comparative genomic analy-
ses of these assemblies led to detection of genes related to agronomic and pathogen-resistance traits, structural
variants, and elucidation of the evolution and phylogenetic relationship among Triticum and Aegilops species
[17–19].
   Long-read DNA sequencing technologies and better assembly algorithms and pipelines have resulted in genome
assemblies with chromosome-scale contiguity [28–31]. Genome assemblies constructed using Pacific Biosciences
circular consensus sequencing (CCS), also known as high-fidelity (HiFi) reads, are advantageous across many
metrics [32–35]. For instance, ~ 20X HiFi coverage can produce highly contiguous and complete genome assem-
blies, while coverage of ~ 4-5X suffices to capture most gene sequences [35]. Several plant genome assemblies,
including Ae. tauschii [15], einkorn (T. monococcum) [36], maize (Zea mays) [37], and barley (Hordeum vulgare)
[35], have achieved chromosome-scale pseudomolecules by combining HiFi reads with long-range scaffolding
optical maps (e.g., Bionano Genomics). Optical maps order and orient contigs, and identify chimeric contigs and
large structural variations to improve the quality and contiguity of assemblies [38–40]. High-throughput chromo-
some conformation capture (Hi-C), a method originally developed to capture long-range chromatin interactions,
has been broadly applied to obtain chromosome-level reference genome assemblies [19, 20, 22, 41].
   Transcriptome datasets obtained from RNA sequencing (RNASeq) data serve as quantitative and qualita-
tive expression evidence for gene annotation. Long-read isoform sequencing (IsoSeq) captures full-length gene
sequences with high accuracy, thus overcoming some of the limitations of RNASeq. Integrating RNASeq and
IsoSeq significantly improves genome annotation [36, 42].
   The production of high-quality genomic assemblies and annotations of Aegilops species remains a significant
endeavor, primarily owing to the large size, and high repeat content (> 80%) of their genomes. Here, chromo-
some-level reference genome assemblies of Ae. comosa, Ae. markgrafii, Ae. umbellulata and Ae. uniaristata were
generated through the integrated use of PacBio HiFi reads, Bionano optical maps and Hi-C scaffolding. Repeat
elements were identified and structural and functional annotation of protein-coding genes were performed. With
the first assemblies of Ae. markgrafii and Ae. uniaristata, this work completes the availability of high-quality
genome assemblies of all diploid Aegilops species. This comprehensive resource is being capitalized upon to
understand the relationships amongst Aegilops and Triticum genomes and subgenomes and to isolate novel alleles
for wheat improvement.

Methods

Plant material and DNA preparation

The plant material consisted of four diploid Aegilops species, namely Ae. comosa accession IPK-AE783, Ae.
markgrafii accession EKC105_RL5723, Ae. umbellulata accession IPK-AE740, and Ae. uniaristata accession
IPK-AE680. Seeds of Ae. umbellulata, Ae. comosa, and Ae. uniaristata were obtained from the Leibniz Insti-
tute of Plant Genetics and Crop Plant Research (IPK), Gatersleben, Germany, while Ae. markgrafii seeds were
sourced from Dr. Eric Kerber (AAFC, Canada). The EKC_RL line of Ae. markgrafii was originally sourced from
Plant Gene Resources of Canada (PGRC) and that it corresponds to accession CN 34579 (formerly PGR 6682).
One plant of each accession was selfed with the heads bagged for a minimum of three generations to increase
homozygosity prior to whole genome sequencing. For each accession, 5 to 68 seedlings were grown in a growth
cabinet under a 16-h photoperiod with 24 °C daytime and 18 °C nighttime temperatures. Prior to sampling, plants
were kept in the dark for 2–3 days. For each genotype, leaf tissue from plants at the 3–5 leaf stage were sampled,
frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen and stored in liquid nitrogen until DNA extraction. Seedlings were allowed
to regrow for subsequent samplings. High-molecular-weight genomic DNA (HMW gDNA) was extracted from
~ 15 g leaf tissue using a modified nuclei extraction protocol (Additional file 3: Sect. 1).

HiFi Library construction and genome sequencing

Approximately 90 µg of HMW gDNA at ~ 100–200 ng/µl per sample was sent to the Centre d’expertise et de
services Génome Québec (Montréal, QC, Canada) for library construction and sequencing. DNA quality control,
SMRT library preparation procedures and sequencing on a PacBio Sequel II instrument are described (Additional
file 3: Sect. 2).

Bionano optical mapping

A total of 200 seeds, harvested from the 5–68 plants sampled from each of the sequenced species, were sent
to the CNRGV Plant Genomic Center (Institut National de Recherche pour l’agriculture, l’alimentation et
l’environnement (INRAE), Auzeville, France) for high-molecular weight genomic DNA isolation, library prepa-
ration and optical mapping on the Saphyr™ system (Bionano Genomics, San Diego, USA).

Hi-C library construction and sequencing

 Hi-C libraries were prepared following the protocol described in Padmarasu et al. [43] as detailed in Additional
file 3: Sect. 3. The cross-linked chromatin DNA was digested using DpnII. The multi-step process included
biotinylation, blunt-end repair, extraction, purification, quantitation, shearing, size distribution estimation, end
repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, purification and PCR amplification. The PCR products were then purified, size-
selected, quantified prior to quality control, which was performed using the KAPA Illumina library quantification
kit. The Hi-C libraries were individually indexed and pooled 3–4 per lane for high-throughput sequencing on
Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end (PE150) reads.

Genome size estimation

The genome size of the species was estimated by flow cytometry (Additional file 3: Sect. 4). Genome sizes were
measured on three different days for accuracy. The flow cytometer List Mode Data (LMD) files were processed
on R Studio using the flowPloidy package [44] to estimate the genome sizes.

Genome assembly

 The raw data generated from the PacBio Sequel II instrument were processed using SMRT Link Analysis
v11.0.0.146107 pipeline. HiFi reads were generated from the raw subreads during the CCS steps using the param-
eters ccs --min-passes 3 --hifi-kinetics --num-threads 24. The CCS bam files were indexed and converted into
fastq using bam2fastq (SmrtLinkv11.0, with default settings) and assembled using hifiasm v0.15.4-r347 with
parameter -l0 [45]. The generated contigs were scaffolded with the Bionano genome maps using the hybridScaf-
fold.pl script of the Bionano Solve pipeline v3.7 with default config file (hybridScaffold_DLE1_config.xml).
Paired-end Hi-C reads were utilized to orient, order and anchor the assembled contigs into pseudochromosome-
level assemblies using YaHS [46]. The contact matrix were visualized and manual curation was performed using
juicertools and Juicebox [47]. Assembly statistics were calculated using custom Perl scripts and the BioKit tool
[48]. Completeness of the Aegilops genome assemblies was evaluated using the Benchmarking Universal Single-
Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) [49] genome mode with MetaEuk v5.34c21f2 [50] and hmmsearch v3.1 [51] against
the Embryophyta odb10 database.

Sample collection and RNA preparation

For each species, a total of 45 plants were grown in 72-cell root trainers in a growth chamber using a 16-h pho-
toperiod with 24 °C daytime and 18 °C nighttime temperatures. Details regarding the seed source are provided
(Additional file 1: Table S1).
   Three biological replicates of the following ten tissue samples were collected from each species: root before
vernalization (RBV), root after vernalization (RAV), crown+leaves before vernalization (CLBV), crown+leaves
after vernalization (CLAV), head at anthesis and two days after anthesis (H), stem+flag leaf at anthesis and
two days after anthesis (SF), developing seeds at 4–9 (DS4-9), 10–15 (DS10-15), 16–21 (DS16-21) and 22–27
(DS22-17) days after anthesis (DAA) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1 and Table S2). Sampling is described (Additional
file 3: Sect. 5). The term “tissue” is being used for all samples although some represent multiple tissue types
(e.g., developing seeds = endosperm+pericarp+embryo). In total, RNA was extracted from 360 tissue samples
representing 4 species × 10 tissues × 3 replicates × 3 samples/replicate (Additional file 1: Table S3).
   RNA extraction procedures are detailed in Additional file 3: Sect. 6. Briefly, total RNA from the 216 non-seed
samples was extracted with the Qiagen RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Toronto, ON, Canada). For the 144 developing
seed samples, RNA was extracted following the protocol described in White et al. [52] with modifications as
detailed in Additional file 3: Sect. 6. Quality control and quantification were carried out (Additional file 3: Sect.
7). The final 120 RNA samples were created by combining an equimolar amount of RNA from three high quality
RNA subsamples of each replicate. The total RNA samples were then diluted to 50 ng/µl for the RBV, CLBV,
RAV, CLAV, H, SF tissues or 40 ng/µl for the developing seed tissues (DS4-9, DS10-15, DS16-21, DS22-27).

RNASeq

 One hundred and twenty samples of total RNA were sent to the Centre d’expertise et de services Génome Québec
(Montreal, QC, Canada) for mRNA sequencing library construction and RNASeq. Prior to library construction,
the service provider confirmed the quality of total RNA using a LabChip GXII (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA,
USA). The concentration of all samples provided exceeded their quality (RIN > 6.5) and quantity (500 ng at 30
ng/µl) standards. Protocols are detailed in Additional file 3: Sect. 8. The libraries were randomized and sequenced
on three NovaSeq 6000 S4 lanes using the 150-bp paired-end (PE150) mode (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).
Adaptors were removed using Trim Galore v0.6.7 (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) and quality
checks were performed using FastQC v0.11.9 (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC) and MultiQC v1.12 [53].
The clean reads were mapped to their respective assemblies using STAR v2.7.10a [54], sorted according to their
coordinates, and indexed using SAMtools v1.9 [55] for visualization by IGV v2.11.6 [56].

IsoSeq

For IsoSeq, equimolar amounts of total RNA from all 30 samples (10 tissues × 3 replicates) from each species were
combined to create RNA pools totalling 2,400 ng at 100 ng/µl. These pools were sent to the Centre d’expertise et
de services Génome Québec for library construction and sequencing. From each RNA pool, one standard and one
long insert size libraries were constructed (Additional file 3: Sect. 9). Each library was sequenced on a PacBio
Sequel II instrument using SMRT Cell 8 M and 24-h movies with a 2-h pre-extension time. The subreads were
converted to CCSs using the ccs algorithm of PacBio SMRT Link Software v12.0.0 (Pacific Bioscience, Menlo
Park, CA, USA) with parameters: ccs --min-passes 3 --num-threads 24. The CCS bam files were pre-processed
and combined using the IsoSeq3 pipeline (lima v2.4.0, refine v3.4.0, cluster v3.4.0; ​h​t​t​p​s​:​/​/​g​i​t​h​u​b​.​c​o​m​/​P​a​c​i​f​i​c​
B​i​o​s​c​i​e​n​c​e​s​/​I​s​o​S​e​q​​​​) to generate high-quality full-length non-chimeric (HQ-FLNC) transcripts with accuracy ≥
99% and ≥ 2 FLNC read support. To generate gene model predictions, the HQ-FLNC transcript sequences were
mapped to their respective assemblies using minimap2 [57] with the following parameters: -ax splice -t 30 -uf
--secondary = no -C5. The non-redundant gene models were obtained from the HQ-FLNC primary alignments
and assembled genomes using the Transcriptome Annotation by Modular Algorithms (TAMA) program suite’s
collapse tool (https://github.com/GenomeRIK/tama.git) [58]. Transcript models with 5’-degraded ends and ​i​s​o​f​o​

Genome annotation

Genome annotation is an intricate process that begins with the characterization and precise determination of the
physical positions of genomic features within DNA sequences, including repeat elements, protein-coding and
non-coding genes. The subsequent functional annotation assigns biological descriptions to the identified struc-
tural features. A detailed workflow describing the computational tools and parameters is depicted in Additional
file 1: Fig. S2 and described in more detail below.

Repeat sequence annotation and insertion time analysis

Annotation of repeat sequences was performed using a de novo repeat library created using the Extensive De novo
Transposable Elements annotator (EDTA v2.2.1) pipeline [59] (parameters: --overwrite 1 --sensitive 1 --anno 1
--evaluate 1–cds --curatedlib). The TREP database [60] was used as a curated library to maintain consistent
naming and classification of TEs. EDTA integrates a number of best-performing structure and homology-based
detection programs, including LTRharvest [61], LTR_FINDER [62], LTR_retriever [63], Generic Repeat Finder
(GRF) [64], TIR-Learner [65], HelitronScanner [66], AnnoSINE [67], RepeatModeler [68] and RepeatMasker
[69], to annotate the predominant transposable element (TE) classes found in plant genomes. To exclude genic
regions from the TE masking, the gene models predicted from the IsoSeq long reads using the TAMA collapse
tool [58] were provided as BED files, and published gene coding sequences (CDS) of Ae. umbellulata TA1851
and Ae. comosa were provided as FASTA files. The output of EDTA comprised the TE counts and their relative
genomic proportions. The centromere positions of the pseudochromosomes were determined by analyzing the
density distribution of Class-I long terminal repeat (LTR) Ty3/Gypsy family Cereba and Quinta elements, identi-
fied through BLAST searches against the TE annotation results [70]. The 5′ and 3′ long-terminal repeats (LTRs)
of intact LTR retrotransposons, identified with LTR_retriever v2.9.9 [63] within EDTA, were utilized to estimate
insertion time (T) using the formula, T = Ks/2r, where K is the divergence between the two LTRs and r is the rate
of nucleotide substitutions per site per year. A substitution rate of 1.3 × 10− 8 was applied [71, 72] .

Annotation of protein-coding genes

 Structural gene annotation was carried out using a strategy combining ab initio gene prediction, transcriptomic
evidence and homology-based prediction. Bedtools v2.30 [73] was used to softmask the genomes with TEs iden-
tified using EDTA in preparation for annotating protein-coding genes. For ab initio gene prediction, AUGUSTUS
v3.3.2 [74] using BRAKER v3.0.3 [75, 76] was employed with the guiding hints generated from spliced-aligned
RNASeq reads and a dataset of annotated proteins constructed as described in Additional file 3: Sect. 10. Pre-
dicted protein sequences from the IsoSeq TAMA collapse non-redundant set of transcripts were obtained using
the GeneMarkS-T algorithm [77] with default parameters. TSEBRA v1.0.3 [78] was utilized to merge and select
gene predictions from BRAKER with RNASeq data and protein modes, and GeneMarkS-T into a non-redundant
gene sets.
     For transcriptome evidence, an improved hybrid reference-based assembly pipeline from StringTie2 v2.1.7
[79] was employed using IsoSeq and RNASeq data (Additional file 3: Sect. 11). For homology-based gene
predictions, annotated HC and LC predicted proteins form Ae. tauschii [16], Ae. speltoides, Ae. longissima and
Ae. sharonensis [17, 18, 21] were downloaded to create a non-redundant protein database using CD-HIT v4.7
(parameter: -c 95) [80]. This database was subsequently mapped against the genome assemblies using Miniprot
v0.6 [81]. Only the top-ranked predictions without frameshift or in-frame stop codons were retained. The predic-
tions from TSEBRA, transcriptomic evidence and homology-based approaches were integrated using EVidenc-
eModeler (EVM) [82] to obtain the consensus gene model sets representing the protein-coding genes. Confidence
classifications of predicted gene models were carried out similarly to previously described protocols [17, 83]
(Additional file 1: Table S4 and Additional file 3: Sect. 12). BUSCO against the embryophyta_odb10 database
was employed in protein mode to assess the completeness of the gene annotations. The functional annotation of
HC and LC predicted protein sequences was performed using the Automatic Assignment of Human-Readable
Descriptions (AHRD) pipeline [84] which uses BLASTp to assign function and the Gene Ontology (GO) data-
base to assign GO terms [85]. AHRD assigns a three-character sequence quality code for each protein where each
character is denoted as “*” when the corresponding criteria are fulfilled or ‘-’ when they are not.

Whole genome comparison, genome synteny and gene duplication analyses

Minimap2 was utilized to align the pseudochromosome assemblies of diploid Aegilops with previously published
Aegilops reference genomes. The alignment results were visualized as dot plots created using D-GENIES [86].
Collinear blocks (regions containing > 5 collinear genes) between pseudochromosomes of genome assemblies
were identified using MCScanX [87] using an all-vs-all BLAST alignment (with default settings) of the protein-
coding sequences. The DupGen_finder-unique.pl module of the DupGen_finder [88], with default parameters,
was utilized to identify various gene duplication modes with Hordeum vulgare as a reference outgroup species.

Orthogroups identification and phylogenetic analysis

Orthofinder v2.5.5 [89] was employed to identify the shared orthogroups among Aegilops and Triticum using
Hordeum vulgare as an outgroup. The HC proteins of 17 diploid Aegilops accessions representing ten species,
T. monococcum TA299 and TA10622, T. urartu G1812, T. turgidum ssp. dicoccoides Zavitan v1.0, T. timophee-
vii PI94760, T. aestivum Chinese Spring (CS) RefSeq v2.1 [90] and Hordeum vulgare MorexV3 [35] were
used. For the polyploid Triticum genomes, the HC proteins from their diploid subgenomes (A, B or D) were
extracted, retaining only the largest protein coding isoforms. Phylogenetic analysis was carried out using the
protein sequences from single-copy orthogroups obtained using Orthofinder, i.e. groups comprising exactly one
protein sequence for each diploid genome (species) or diploid subgenome in the case of polyploid species. Each
single-copy orthogroup was aligned using MAFFT v7.5.20 with default parameters [91]. Gblocks v0.91b [92]
was utilized to trim ambiguous regions within each alignment using parameters -t = p -b5 = h. The alignments

Results

Genome sequencing, assembly and size estimation

A hybrid approach, integrating PacBio HiFi sequencing, Bionano optical mapping and Hi-C data was imple-
mented to produce chromosome-level reference assemblies of Ae. comosa, Ae. markgrafii, Ae. uniaristata, and
Ae. umbellulata. Between 143 and 195 Gb HiFi reads with an average CCS length of 13,988 − 15,769 bp resulted
in an estimated genome coverage of 28.1-35.8X (Additional file 1: Table S5). Hybrid scaffolding with the Bion-
ano optical map resulted in scaffolds with improved contiguity. Finally, chromosomal-level scaffolds were con-
structed by orienting and ordering hybrid scaffolds using 43 to 91X paired-end Hi-C data generated for each
Aegilops species (Additional file 1: Table S5). For instance, Hi-C scaffolding, as depicted in Fig. 1A contact
matrix, assigned ~ 99% of the Ae. markgrafii genome of accession EKC105_RL5723 to seven pseudomolecules.
The chromosome-level genome assemblies produced herein ranged in size from 3.58 to 4.48 Gb (Table 1), which
is comparable to the previously published diploid Aegilops species (4.02–6.7 Gb) [16–19, 21]. Methods and
assembly statistics have been summarized for all available diploid Aegilops reference genomes (Additional file
1: Table S6).
   The Ae. comosa IPK-AE783 assembly of 4.41 Gb with contig N90 and N50 of 50.61 and 172.45 Mb, respec-
tively, compared advantageously with the recently published assembly of Ae. comosa PI551049 which had an
contig N50 of 23.59 Mb [20]. Similarly, our assembly of Ae. umbellulata IPK-AE740 had contig N90 values
(12.05 M) that exceeded the recently reported Ae. umbellulata TA1851 (5.18 Mb) [22] and PI 554,389 (8.79 Mb)
[23]. Overall, the contig N50 and N90 values of diploid Aegilops assemblies produced herein using PacBio HiFi
and Bionano optical map exceed the contiguity of Aegilops assemblies published to date (Fig. 1B and Additional
file 1: Table S6). The seven pseudomolecules of the four Aegilops assemblies incorporated 96.4 to 99.9% of the
scaffolds, with the remaining being assigned to a “Chromosome Unknown” (ChrUn) that represents the concat-
enated unmapped scaffolds not assigned to pseudomolecules (Table 1 and Additional file 1: Table S7). The com-
pleteness of the genome assemblies, evaluated using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO)
[49, 98] in genome mode indicated 97.8 to 98.3% completeness of 1,614 single-copy embryophyte genes (Fig. 1C
and Additional file 2: Table S8). The nuclear DNA content of each species estimated by flow cytometry ranged
from 4.3 to 5.4 Gb (Table 1), which is consistent with previous estimates of 4.4-6.0 Gb for Aegilops diploid
genomes [18, 99].

Annotation and analysis of transposable elements

Approximately 3.1–3.9 Gb of repeat elements, representing 86.3–88.2% of the assemblies, were annotated. Class-
I long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons were the most abundant, with Ty1/Copia and Ty3/Gypsy super-
families representing the largest fractions, accounting for ~ 17.4–23.3% and 39.5–47.1%, respectively (Table 2),
similar to the other Aegilops species annotated to date (Fig. 2A). Ty3/Gypsy were enriched in the centromeric
regions, and their density was inversely correlated with the gene density, while Ty1/Copia tended to be more
uniformly distributed along chromosomes (Fig. 2B, shown for the representative assembly of Ae. markgrafii
EKC105_RL5723; the other three species are presented in Additional file 1: Fig.S3).

umbellulata [100] and often detected within protein-coding regions, represented 1.1–1.3% of the genome of the
four Aegilops assemblies [101]. CACTA transposons covered 7.5–11.2% of the assemblies, comparable to the
previously published Aegilops assemblies (5.1–17.1%). The consistency was also true for other Class II DNA
transposons such as Mutator, Tc1/Mariner and Helitrons [16–19]. Both SINEs and CACTA families showed an
enrichment in distal chromosomal regions and their distribution pattern directly corelated with gene densities
(Fig. 2B and Additional file 1: Fig. S3). The Ty3/Gypsy elements Cereba and Quinta are enriched in centromeric
regions but absent from the rest of the genome [70, 102], and analysis of their density distribution along the chro-
mosomes identified the centromeric regions in the assemblies (Fig. 2B and Additional file 1: Fig. S3). The 45 S
rDNA content in the four assemblies was low (0.00-0.04%), and no 45 S rDNA was annotated in Ae. umbellulata.

Orthology and multi-gene phylogenetic analyses revealed close relationship of M, C, N and U genomes
within the D-lineage

The orthology analysis using Orthofinder [89] revealed both shared and unique orthogroups across species by
comparing annotated proteins from our and the previously published diploid Aegilops and polyploid Triticum
genome annotations (Additional file 1: Table S17). Of the 1,692,184 proteins from 17 Aegilops genomes, nine
Triticum subgenomes and the H. vulgare genome, 1,554,113 (91.8%) clustered into 96,381 orthogroups, of which
8,349 were considered core because they included at least one or more proteins from each of the 27 genomes or
subgenomes analyzed (Fig. 4A). Of this core set, 2,706 orthogroups contained exactly one orthologous copy in
each genome or subgenome.
   To determine the relationships among Aegilops and Triticum species, a maximum-likelihood (ML) phylo-
genetic tree was generated with best-fit JTT + F+I+R6 model using a concatenated matrix of these 2,706 core
single-copy orthogroups, using H. vulgare as an outgroup. Three major clades, corresponding to the A, B and D
lineages were observed (Fig. 4B). All Aegilops species belonged to the D-lineage, except Ae. speltoides which
clustered with the B-lineage. The remaining four species of the Sitopsis section, namely Ae. searsii, Ae. bicornis,
Ae. longissima and Ae. sharonensis clustered within the D-lineage clade. This clade also includes the recently
published genomes of Ae. comosa PI 551,049 [20] and Ae. umbellulata TA1851 [22].

Discussion

The potential of Aegilops species for wheat improvement has attracted scientific community for more than a
century [10, 12, 107]. Despite their rich genetic diversity, Aegilops species remain largely underutilized. The
development of robust genomic resources and improved molecular tools are expected to assist in unlocking their
full potential [108]. The generation of high-quality, chromosome-level reference genome assemblies and compre-
hensive annotation of our study expands our understanding of the genomic resources of four additional diploid
Aegilops species: Ae. comosa, Ae. markgrafii, Ae. umbellulata and Ae. uniaristata. The completion of all 11
diploid Aegilops genomes constitutes a milestone towards the study of the remaining polyploid genomes within


                                                                                                          BMC Plant Biology
https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-10046-2                                                         Article in Press

this genus. High contiguity and completeness of the assemblies were achieved through a hybrid sequencing and
scaffolding approach that integrated PacBio HiFi sequencing, Bionano optical mapping and Hi-C. The assembly
sizes, ranging from 3.58 to 4.48 Gb, align well with the reported sizes for other diploid Aegilops genomes [15,
17–19, 22, 23], T. urartu (4.86 Gb) [109], and Hordeum vulgare (4.2 Gb) [35], although they are consistently
smaller than the flow cytometry estimates, a typical phenomenon in large grass genomes [18, 110]. Highly repeti-
tive and duplicated chromosomal DNA sequences may account for the possible flow cytometry overestimations
[18, 111, 112]. Moreover, both inter- and intra-specific variability in genome sizes have been reported for Aegil-
ops species [99], and genome size differences of up to 15% were reported [113].
   Transposable elements (TEs) make up more than 80% of Triticum and Aegilops species genomes [18, 70, 114–
116]. Although the total proportion of TE content was relatively stable across the four diploid Aegilops species
(86.3–88.2%), the observed differential distribution of Ty3/Gypsy and Ty1/Copia elements, and the enrichment
of SINE and CACTA elements in distal chromosomal regions are consistent with patterns seen in other monocot
genomes [15, 109, 117–119]. The higher Ty3/Gypsy:Ty1/Copia ratio in wheat [120] and Aegilops [17–20, 22]
reflects the differential accumulation of TE families across these genomes. Differences observed in TEs across
assemblies could be biological, but could also result from differences in annotation protocols. For example,
2.45% CACTA were annotated in Ae. umbellulata TA1851 using the EDTA pipeline without the TREP database
[22], but with the latter, we annotated 9.91% CACTA in Ae. umbellulata IPK-AE740. Consequently, the use of
TREP database has methodological consequences that extend beyond repeat quantification and propagates into
gene prediction. Repeat elements such as CACTA often carry transposase-coding domains and captured host
gene fragments, and incomplete annotation of this superfamily leaves coding-like sequence unmasked, and are
readily predicted as protein-coding genes by ab initio methods. The integration of a curated Triticeae repeat
library therefore increase the fraction of the assemblies to be soft-masked prior to gene annotation and reduced
the likelihood of TE-derived gene overprediction. Resolving rDNA arrays in Aegilops will require ultra-long
ONT reads or targeted assembly strategies, as demonstrated for bread wheat [121], and these regions remain
recognized gap in current Triticeae references.
   The recent bursts of Ty1/Copia and Ty3/Gypsy LTR TEs in Ae. comosa and Ae. markgrafii illustrate the dynamic
structural evolution of these genomes [70, 122], as potentially driven by environmental pressures [123] or specia-
tion events [124]. In contrast, the broad peaks of these LTR TEs in Ae. uniaristata and Ae. umbellulata indicate
a more gradual insertion accumulation process in these species [70]. These differences in transpositional activity
of TE families could be species-specific to a large extent [125].
   The integration of high-accuracy long-read IsoSeq and deep sequence coverage RNASeq data proved crucial
for robust gene prediction and annotation quality as exemplified by the comprehensive summary of gene annota-
tion methods and results for diploid Aegilops (Additional file 1: Table S18) and the distribution of the annotated
protein-coding HC genes on scaffolds (Additional file 2: Table S14). IsoSeq produced high-quality full-length
cDNA sequences that accurately map intron-exon structures and flanking untranslated regions without the need
for assembly [126], as demonstrated in several plant genomes [127–129]. Several recent studies have demon-
strated the combined use of IsoSeq and RNASeq to improve the accuracy of gene annotation and gain compre-
hensive insights into the extent and the diversity of isoforms [130–133]. Functional annotation using AHRD [84]
provided qualitative descriptions of the final annotated set of protein-coding genes, as previously reported for
diploid Aegilops [17], barley [134], and lablab [135]. The high BUSCO completeness metrics demonstrate com-
parable or superior gene completeness compared to published diploid Aegilops assembly annotations, uphold-
ing the importance of combining evidence-based (IsoSeq + RNASeq) and ab initio methods to achieve quality
annotations.
   Unravelling the phylogeny and genome composition of polyploid Triticeae species remains challenging due
to the abundance of repeat DNA sequences, frequent hybridizations and complex evolutionary histories [136].
Advanced genomic approaches can overcome these challenges. Here orthology and phylogenetic analyses were
used to depict the evolutionary relationships among Aegilops and Triticum species. With the exception of Ae.
speltoides within the B-lineage, the clustering of Aegilops species within the D-lineage is in agreement with their

BMC Plant Biology
Article in Press                                                    https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-10046-2

shared morphological traits [137]. This clear separation of Ae. speltoides from the other Sitopsis species aligns
with earlier studies [138–140] and with recent findings from transcriptome-based and whole-genome molecu-
lar phylogenies [17, 18, 141, 142]. The tree topology revealed that the four newly sequenced diploid Aegilops
reported herein, i.e., Ae. comosa, Ae. markgrafii, Ae. umbellulata and Ae. uniaristata, alongside published Ae.
comosa [20] and Ae. umbellulata [22, 23] genomes, formed a sister monophyletic clade within the strongly-
supported D-lineage clade. This corroborated previous findings indicating close relationships between the C and
U genomes, as well as between the M and N genomes [1, 141–143].
   Comparative genomic analysis, including gene duplication and synteny, provides insights into the structural
evolution and gene content dynamics of the genomes [144, 145]. Gene duplication, a driver of neo-functionaliza-
tion, provides raw genetic material crucial for both adaptation and crop improvement [88, 146, 147]. Duplicated
gene pairs within the four diploid Aegilops species were identified and categorized as follows: whole genome
duplicate (WGD), tandem duplicate (TD), proximal duplicate (PD), transposed duplicate (TRD) and dispersed
duplicate (DSD) [88]. The predominance of DSD suggests that these duplication events were more common over
the course of evolution, and contributed significantly to gene duplication and genome evolution [148]. The pre-
dominance of DSD genes has also been reported in the Ae. tauschii genome [149].
   Synteny analysis, comparing gene orders across genomes, identifies structural variants resulting from the evo-
lution of taxonomic groups [150]. Chromosomal rearrangements disrupt the collinearity, and thus can hinder
introgressions of wild alleles through interspecific hybridization [151, 152]. The multiple intra- and inter-chro-
mosomal rearrangements detected herein underscore the dynamic evolution of the Aegilops genomes, hinting at
their potential role(s) in impeding the efficient transfer of genetic diversity from these Aegilops wild relatives to
wheat (Triticum species) [153]. Both chromosome rearrangements and introgressions are significant in the evo-
lution of the C, M, N, and U genomes [154]. By applying fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) with bulked
oligos, Li et al. [105] confirmed rearrangements in chromosomes 2, 4, 6 and 7 of these four genomes.
   Chromosome-by-chromosome DNA sequence analyses of Ae. umbellulata and Ae. comosa assemblies
revealed intra-specific structural rearrangements, such as an inversion in chromosome 1U between IPK-AE740
and TA1851 [22] and in chromosomes 3 M (distal end) and 4 M (center) between IPK-AE783 and PI 551,049
[20]. These intra-specific structural rearrangements could represent actual, yet unconfirmed structural variants
or could result from assembly artefacts, as the accessions compared were assembled using different sequencing
platforms and bioinformatics pipelines, and raw data from the published assemblies were unavailable for uni-
form reprocessing. Orthogonal validation, for example by cytogenetic mapping by oligo-FISH painting [105],
would be required to confirm the observed structural variations. Additionally, differences in naming conven-
tions of chromosome 4U and 6U complicate direct comparisons across studies. For instance, Abrouk et al. [22]
assigned chromosome numbers of Ae. umbellulata TA1851 based on homology with the D genome of Ae. taus-
chii AL8/78 [15, 16], whereas Singh et al. [23] followed the D subgenome of CS RefSeq v2.1 [90] and centro-
meric homoeology evidence [155, 156]. Although the D genome of Ae. tauschii and the D subgenome of bread
wheat are equivalent in origin, differences in reference genomes and assembly annotation strategies can lead to
discrepancies in chromosome assignments. In our study, assembly and chromosome naming was based on Hi-C
contact synteny with Ae. tauschii AL8/78 [15, 16], a close relative of Ae. umbellulata, to ensure consistency. This
approach provides a more robust framework for interpreting structural variation and evolutionary relationships
among Aegilops species.


Conclusions

The high-quality reference genome assemblies of Ae. comosa, Ae. markgrafii, Ae. umbellulata, and Ae. unia-
ristata, generated using a hybrid approach combining PacBio HiFi sequencing, Bionano optical mapping and
chromosome-level scaffolding using Hi-C data, have expanded our understanding of the genomes of Aegilops
species. These reference assemblies display improved contiguity and completeness over previous Aegilops

our understanding of genome complexity and diversity through comparative genomics, to exploit these gene
